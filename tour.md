Welcome to the neighborhood of Los Feliz, in the City of Los Angeles.  Nearly a century ago these blocks were the cradle of a dream. Here, in small, rented rooms and a backyard garage, Walt and Roy O. Disney built the foundations of what would become one of the most influential entertainment studios in the world.

This tour will take you to the places where it all began – addresses you might pass without a second glance, yet each holds a piece of history. From the family home where Walt first unpacked his drawing board, to the storefront where the Disney Brothers Cartoon Studio opened for business, to the Hyperion studio lot where Mickey Mouse whistled for the first time – these sites tell a story of persistence, imagination, and a little bit of luck.

As you walk, try to see these streets as Walt and Roy once did: a patchwork of opportunity and challenge, where every sketch, every handshake, and every late-night idea brought them closer to changing animation forever. The landmarks you’ll visit aren’t grand monuments – they’re ordinary buildings that once held extraordinary dreams. But in Los Feliz, those dreams still echo in the walls.

In early October of 1923, Kansas City natives Walt and Roy O. Disney signed a lease to rent the back room in the offices of the McRae & Abernathy Holly-Vermont Real Estate Company at 4651 Kingswell Ave.  On October 16th, they signed their first distribution deal, and the Disney Brothers Cartoon Studio was born.  Little could the brothers have known that their small, but ambitious studio, would one day become one of Hollywood’s biggest success stories.

The brothers immediately set to work producing the Alice Comedies, a series of short films which blended animation and live-action footage, distributed by Margaret Winkler, a notable New York film distributor.

Actress Virginia Davis initially played Alice, a young girl with a vivid imagination, who found her daydreams spring to life in the form of bouncing jovial cartoons.  Walt and Roy would film their live-action segments on the streets and alleys of Los Feliz, including the sidewalk you’re standing on right now.

In early 1924, after gaining momentum and income from the Alice series, the brothers upgraded into a slightly larger space, just next door at 4649 Kingswell Ave.

This space witnessed key milestones: hiring their first animator, Rollin “Ham” Hamilton; welcoming Ub Iwerks to the team; and the fateful moment when Walt met his future wife, ink and paint artist Lillian Bounds – an encounter that would change his personal and professional life.

As you stand here, feel the hum of creativity in the walls. From this humble office, the earliest Alice Comedies short films were crafted and sent out for eventual distribution across the country. Every stroke, every line, every painted frame born here laid the foundation for what would become one of the world’s most beloved brands.

Now you’re standing in front of 4637 Melbourne Avenue, Walt and Lillian Disney’s first home together – a modest one-bedroom apartment. This building – little changed over 100 years – captures the texture of the neighborhood that nurtured the Disney dream. This part of Los Feliz, then often called “Hollymont,” was a budding creative enclave where Walt, Lillian, and their small team lived, breathed, and invented.

Just blocks from their studio and homes, this area brimmed with apartments, bungalows, markets, and cafes. It formed the backdrop of late-night sketches, quick story revisions, shared dreams, and coffee breaks between shoots. As you walk through the block, imagine friendly nods between animators, quick bursts of laughter after deadlines, and whispering ideas passing through apartment windows. The neighborhood itself was a collaborator – quiet, comforting, and full of inspiration.

Welcome to 4406 Kingswell Avenue – a modest, Craftsman-style bungalow that feels frozen in time. Imagine Walt Disney, wide-eyed and determined, stepping off a train at La Grande Station into sunny Los Angeles in mid 1923. With only $40 in his pocket but a vivid vision in his mind, he headed to the Sawtelle Veterans Hospital, where his brother Roy Oliver Disney was recovering from tuberculosis contracted during his service in World War I. Together, the brothers headed up the newly paved Vermont Avenue and sought refuge with their Aunt Charlotte and Uncle Robert.  

In the one-story home’s humble garage Walt and Roy began to bring their animated dreams to life. Surrounded by simple tools and flickering light, he hand-drew his way toward wonder. You can almost sense the scratch of pencil, the rustling of paper, the excited hum of possibilities.

Their stay at “Uncle Robert’s House” wouldn’t last long; as soon as the brothers could, they moved…right across the street to a boarding house, but 4406 Kingswell remains an enduring part of the Disney legacy.

When the house was threatened with demolition in the first part of this century, a member of the Disney family saved the home and restored it to nearly its original condition, as it proudly remains to this day.

Our journey next takes us to 4151 Prospect Avenue, home of The Prospect Studios. At first glance, it may seem just another gated television lot – but this site tells a story almost as old as Disney’s itself.

Prospect began life in 1915 as the Vitagraph Studio, a pioneer in silent films. Later, the studio became Warner Bros. Vitaphone, where experiments with synchronized sound paralleled the same innovations Walt Disney was exploring just down the road with Steamboat Willie.

By the 1940s, the property had transformed again – this time into the west coast headquarters for the American Broadcasting Company, or ABC.   In 1954, Walt launched Disneyland, his first weekly television show, and ABC became its broadcast home. It was a partnership that not only helped finance the building of the Disneyland theme park in Anaheim, California, but also cemented Disney’s presence on television screens across the nation. Millions of families gathered each Sunday night to hear Walt himself introduce new stories – from folktales featuring Davy Crockett to scientific yarns of space-age travel yet to come.  

As the decades rolled on, the Prospect lot remained a hub of American television storytelling and ingenuity. Shows like General Hospital and Grey’s Anatomy were produced on these soundstages. And when The Walt Disney Company purchased ABC in 1996, the circle closed: where Walt Disney once relied on ABC for his future, one of ABC’s oldest studio sites now belonged to the company that bore Walt’s name, outright.

As you look at the gates of Prospect, you’re seeing more than a studio lot. You’re seeing a chapter of entertainment history that connects stories from across Los Feliz with the vast global media landscape we know today.

Here at 2491 and 2495 Lyric Avenue stands a pair of almost-matching houses that Walt and Roy O. Disney built for themselves after graduating from Kingswell, in 1927.  These homes, purchased as kits from the Pacific Ready Cut Homes, not only offered more space for Walt and Roy’s growing families, but it was just a short walk to their new studio space on Hyperion Avenue.

Behind closed doors in the modest garage of Walt’s home at 2495 Lyric, the Disney's also worked in secrecy on a new project that would change the trajectory of their lives forever. With the studio’s creative control over Oswald the Lucky Rabbit slipping away, Walt and his closest, trusted collaborators worked in hushed urgency, often late into the night, to finish the earliest starring vehicles for an all-new cartoon character.

From this small haven of bricks and wood, a new hero emerged – Mickey Mouse – a creation destined to change the world.

You’ve arrived at 2719 Hyperion Avenue – the heart of Disney history for nearly a decade and a half. When Walt and Roy O. Disney moved here in 1926, their team was still small – barely two dozen artists and technicians. But the move signaled ambition. No longer working out of borrowed offices or cramped storefronts, the brothers now had a purpose-built studio space. 

Here, inside cream and white stucco buildings with red-tiled roofs, some of animation’s most important “firsts” came to life. In 1928, Mickey Mouse whistled his way into the world in Steamboat Willie, the first cartoon to feature synchronized sound. Just a few years later, Flowers and Trees introduced audiences to the three-strip Technicolor process in animation, dazzling moviegoers with a world of vibrant hues. The Old Mill in 1937 showcased Disney’s multiplane camera – layers of animation moving independently, creating a breathtaking illusion of depth. Every short, every experiment pushed the art form further.

The culmination of this creative ferment came in 1937 with the premiere of Walt Disney’s Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs – the world’s first full-length animated feature film. Critics of era reportedly referred to the project as ‘Disney’s Folly’ during production, believing no audience would sit through a 90-minute cartoon. But the premiere at the Carthay Circle Theatre proved them all wrong. The film was a sensation from the start, and its success helped to transform Disney from a scrappy studio into a cultural powerhouse.  The success of Snow White helped the Disneys purchase an even larger property just north of here in the city of Burbank – a space The Walt Disney Company still calls home to this day.

In the 1960s, the original Hyperion Avenue studio buildings were razed for a supermarket. While nothing of the original studio remains, the ground you’re standing on once held the beating heart of early animation history as it was being made. Hyperion avenue was where Disney’s identity further crystallized – where a mouse became a star, where shorts grew into features, and where an art form matured into a global industry. Though the buildings are gone, the spirit remains – and if you listen closely, you might just hear echoes of Mickey Mouse’s first whistle floating on the breeze.

You’ve now walked the streets where an empire was born – not in grand towers or lavish studios, but in garages, rented storefronts, and modest neighborhood homes. Here in Los Feliz, Walt and Roy O. Disney learned to dream bigger than their circumstances.

At 4406 Kingswell, Walt first set up shop in his uncle’s garage. Just down the street, the brothers launched their first studio, where ordinary rooms filled with extraordinary energy. Along Hyperion Avenue, their ideas blossomed into cultural landmarks – Mickey Mouse, Technicolor animation, and Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs. And at The Prospect Studios, the partnership with ABC would blossom over the decades to carry Disney stories far beyond Los Angeles.

What ties all of these places together is not just history, but possibility. Walt Disney himself once said, ‘…it was all started by a mouse.’ But in truth, it all started with determination – two brothers who believed in their work enough to risk everything. They weren’t building a media empire; they were simply chasing the next idea, one sketch, one story, one frame at a time.

As you finish this tour, look around at the neighborhood. The streets are still lined with bungalows and storefronts, just as they were a century ago. Ordinary, perhaps – but if you listen closely, you can hear the echoes: the scratch of a pencil, the laughter of artists, the whistle of a mouse. Disney history isn’t only preserved in theme parks or films – it still lives here, in Los Feliz, where imagination first took root.

Thank you for walking this journey. May it remind you that big dreams often begin in the humblest of places – and that the magic of creativity is never far away.
